The Malayan tapir is the only tapir species found in Asia. Its closest relatives live thousands of kilometers away in Central and South America.

A Name Still Under Debate

Tapirs belong to the order Perissodactyla, the odd-toed hoofed mammals, which makes their closest living relatives rhinoceroses and horses. In 2011, the taxonomists Colin Groves and Peter Grubb moved the Malayan tapir into its own genus, Acrocodia, because of its larger size, separate range and distinctive coloring. Some databases now list it as Acrocodia indica, but most sources, including the Mammal Diversity Database, still call it Tapirus indicus.

Physical Profile

The Malayan tapir is the largest of the four tapir species. Adults measure roughly 1.8 to 2.5 m long and about 1 m tall at the shoulder, and usually weigh between 250 and 400 kg. Unusually for a mammal, females tend to be larger than males.

Its coat is easy to recognize: a black head, shoulders and legs, with a large white "saddle" across the back and sides. Its short, flexible trunk, or proboscis, is formed from the nose and upper lip and is the longest of any tapir. It has four toes on each front foot, though only three touch the ground, and three toes on each back foot. Its eyes are small and its eyesight is poor, but its senses of smell and hearing are excellent.

Calves look completely different. They are born with a dark coat covered in white stripes and spots, which fades after about five or six months.

Habitat and Geography

Malayan tapirs live in tropical rainforest, both primary and secondary, as well as swamps, montane forest and forest edges. They stay close to rivers and marshes and can be found up to about 2,000 m in elevation, though they are most common on lower slopes and in valley bottoms.

Their range covers southern Myanmar, southern Thailand, Peninsular Malaysia and the island of Sumatra in Indonesia. What remains of that range is badly fragmented. Malaysia holds the largest population, estimated at 1,500 to 2,000 animals.

Feeding and Diet

The Malayan tapir is a herbivore and a selective browser. It eats leaves, buds, twigs, bark, fruit, grasses and aquatic plants, more than 120 plant species in all, and takes in about 4 to 5 percent of its body weight each day. It uses its trunk to grab plants and pull them into its mouth, and it pushes over or walks down young trees to reach the leaves. Tapirs crave salt and will travel 5 km or more to reach a salt lick.

Many of the seeds a tapir swallows pass through it unharmed and are dropped kilometers away, and some even sprout faster afterward. This makes the tapir an important seed disperser for the rainforest.

Behaviors and Adaptations

Malayan tapirs are mostly solitary. They rest hidden in the forest during the day and feed at dusk, during the night and before dawn. Each one uses a home range of roughly 10 to 25 square kilometers, and the ranges of neighbors overlap.

Tapirs are strong swimmers. They can walk along the bottom of a river and stay underwater for up to about 90 seconds, and they wallow in water and mud to cool off and to keep biting insects away. When threatened, a tapir freezes, then bolts for the nearest water.

The black-and-white coat is a form of camouflage. In the dim forest at night, the pattern breaks up the tapir's outline, so a predator sees what looks like a pale rock instead of an animal. A calf's spots and stripes do the same job, blending in with sunlight on the forest floor.

Communication

Malayan tapirs communicate with high-pitched whistles, clicks and hiccup-like sounds. They use these calls when they are frightened or in pain, as warnings, and during courtship, and a tapir's whistle is often answered by another tapir's.

Scent also matters. Tapirs mark their paths with sprays of urine, and they sniff and touch one another when they meet. During courtship, they sometimes curl back their upper lip in a behavior called flehmen, which helps them pick up scent signals.

Life History Cycle and Breeding Behaviors

Tapirs have one of the longest pregnancies of any land mammal: about 13 months, or 390 to 400 days. The mother almost always gives birth to a single calf, which weighs about 6 to 11 kg. The calf can stand within one or two hours of birth, starts eating solid food within a few weeks, and can swim at about three weeks old.

Calves are weaned at about 6 to 8 months, but they usually stay with their mother for a year or more. Tapirs mature at about 2 to 3 years of age, and females give birth roughly once every 18 months to 2 years. Wild tapirs can live about 30 years.

Predators

Tigers and leopards are the Malayan tapir's main natural predators, and calves are the most at risk. A tapir's thick skin on the back of the head and neck, up to 2.5 cm thick, may help protect it from bites.

The greatest threat comes from people: lowland rainforest is being cleared for palm oil plantations and other farming, and the forest that remains is cut up by roads, where tapirs are often hit by cars. Tapirs are also caught in snares and hunted.

Conservation

On July 11, 2026, Fresno Chaffee Zoo welcomed its first-ever Malayan tapir calf, a male born to mother Ulan and father William. More than 4,000 community votes chose his name: Winston. He now explores the Kingdoms of Asia habitat with his mother.

Fresno Chaffee Zoo is also a member of the Roundtable on Sustainable Palm Oil, which promotes sustainable palm oil to protect the rainforest habitats that Malayan tapirs depend on.
