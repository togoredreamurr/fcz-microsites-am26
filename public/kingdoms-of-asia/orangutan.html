Three orangutan species are now recognized: the Sumatran orangutan (P. abelii), the Bornean orangutan (P. pygmaeus) and the Tapanuli orangutan (P. tapanuliensis), which was described in 2017. This page is about the Sumatran orangutan.

Physical Profile

Sumatran orangutans show strong sexual dimorphism: males are about twice the size of females. Males stand roughly 1.4 m tall and weigh about 90 kg, while females stand roughly 0.9 m and weigh about 45 kg. Their arm span can exceed 2 m.

Their long, shaggy reddish-orange hair is paler and longer than the Bornean species'. Mature flanged males develop wide, fleshy cheek pads and a throat pouch, and Sumatran males often have longer, paler facial hair and beards than Bornean males. Their arms are much longer than their legs, and both hands and feet can grasp.

Habitat and Geography

Sumatran orangutans live in tropical rainforest, including lowland and hill forest and swamp forest, and spend almost their entire lives in the trees.

They are found only in northern Sumatra, Indonesia, mainly in the provinces of Aceh and North Sumatra. Their range is far smaller than the Bornean orangutan's.

Feeding and Diet

Orangutans are primarily frugivores. Fruit makes up most of their diet, and figs are especially important. They also eat leaves, bark, flowers, insects, honey and occasionally small vertebrates. Sumatran orangutans eat more insects and fruit than Bornean orangutans do. They rely on an exceptional mental map of the forest to track which trees are fruiting and when.

Because they spread seeds as they travel and feed, orangutans are major seed dispersers, sometimes called the gardeners of the forest.

Behaviors and Adaptations

Orangutans are the most arboreal of the great apes, and Sumatran orangutans come to the ground even less often than Bornean ones, most likely because Sumatran tigers live in the same forests. They move by quadrumanous clambering, using all four limbs as grasping hands, and they sway trees back and forth to cross gaps in the canopy. Almost every night they build a fresh sleeping nest in the canopy, sometimes with a roof.

They are semi-solitary and far less social than other great apes, though Sumatran orangutans tolerate one another more than Bornean orangutans do.

Orangutans have been documented using sticks to pull insects and seeds out of fruit, and using leaves as gloves, umbrellas and napkins. These tool traditions differ between populations, which is evidence of culture.

Communication

Mature flanged males give the long call, a loud, far-carrying roar amplified by the throat pouch. It advertises the male's location, deters rival males and attracts females.

Other sounds include kiss-squeaks, which orangutans often amplify by holding leaves to their mouths (a form of tool use in communication), along with grumbles and raspberries. Their facial expressions are rich. Mothers and infants communicate through touch and gesture, and their gestural repertoire is extensive and partly learned.

Life History Cycle and Breeding Behaviors

Orangutans have the slowest life history of any mammal other than humans. After a gestation of roughly 8.5 months, a female gives birth to a single infant. She then waits roughly 8 to 9 years before giving birth again, the longest interval between births of any mammal. This is why orangutan populations recover so slowly from losses.

Infants cling to their mother constantly in their first years and stay with her for 6 to 9 years, learning what is safe to eat and how to build nests. Females mature at around 15 years of age, and males later. Males show bimaturism: some stay unflanged for years and may never develop cheek pads while a dominant male is nearby. In the wild, orangutans live roughly 45 to 50 years.

Predators

The Sumatran tiger and the clouded leopard are the main natural predators, which is why Sumatran orangutans stay in the canopy more than Bornean ones. Infants are the most vulnerable.

The greatest threat comes from people: deforestation for palm oil, and the illegal pet trade, in which mothers are typically killed so their infants can be taken.

Conservation

Fresno Chaffee Zoo is an AZA SAFE: Saving Animals From Extinction partner for orangutans, and a member of the Roundtable on Sustainable Palm Oil, which works to reduce the deforestation that is destroying orangutan habitat.
