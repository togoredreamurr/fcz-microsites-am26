Common names

A Disputed Family Tree

Where the Sunda gharial belongs has long been debated. Based on the anatomy of its skull, Tomistoma was traditionally placed with the true crocodiles in the family Crocodylidae. Molecular evidence, however, groups it with the true gharial in the family Gavialidae. Morphology and genetics disagreed, and genetics has largely won, but some sources still list the species under Crocodylidae.

Physical Profile

Sunda gharials grow to roughly 3 to 5 m long. Their most striking feature is a very long, narrow, parallel-sided snout, far narrower than a crocodile's, which is where the name "false gharial" comes from. Unlike the true gharial, males do not develop a bulbous growth on the tip of the snout.

They are dark olive to reddish-brown, with dark blotches and bands across the body and tail. Their long teeth stay visible even when the jaws are closed.

Habitat and Geography

Sunda gharials live in freshwater peat swamps, lowland swamp forest, slow-moving rivers and lakes. They are strongly associated with peat-swamp forest, one of the most threatened habitat types in Southeast Asia.

Today they are found in peninsular Malaysia, Sumatra and Borneo. Historically, their range also included Java, Sulawesi, southern Thailand and Vietnam.

Feeding and Diet

The Sunda gharial is a carnivore. Its narrow snout is a specialist adaptation for catching fish: it meets little water resistance, so it can sweep quickly sideways through the water.

Its diet is broader than its snout suggests. Along with fish and crustaceans, it eats mammals such as macaques and deer, as well as birds and reptiles, and larger adults take proportionally more large prey. Attacks on humans have been recorded but are rare.

Behaviors and Adaptations

Sunda gharials are mainly aquatic and bask on riverbanks to regulate their body temperature. Their narrow snout trades strength for speed: it closes quickly through water but is weaker than a broad snout, which is why their diet leans toward fish.

Their eyes and nostrils sit on top of the head, so they can stay almost fully submerged. A transparent third eyelid protects their eyes underwater, and valves seal the throat so they can open their mouths underwater without flooding the airway. Pressure receptors in the skin of the jaws detect water movements made by prey.

Communication

Crocodilians are among the most vocal reptiles. Their hatchlings call from inside the egg, which prompts the mother to open the nest. Adult crocodilians hiss, growl and bellow, and some species use low-frequency vibrations and infrasound during displays.

Crocodilians also communicate with chemical signals from glands in the jaw and cloaca, and with visual signals such as body posture and head-slapping at the water's surface.

Life History Cycle and Breeding Behaviors

The Sunda gharial is a mound-nesting species. The female builds a nest mound from leaf litter and vegetation, and heat from the decomposing plants helps warm the eggs. A clutch holds roughly 20 to 60 eggs, which incubate for roughly 70 to 100 days. Unusually for a crocodilian, parental care after hatching appears to be limited.

Like other crocodilians, the Sunda gharial has temperature-dependent sex determination: the temperature of the nest decides whether hatchlings are male or female. This makes the species directly vulnerable to a warming climate.

Predators

Adults have few natural predators. Eggs and hatchlings, however, are taken by monitor lizards, wild pigs, civets, birds and fish.

The greatest threats come from people: draining swamps for palm oil and pulpwood plantations, drowning in fishing nets, and egg collection.
